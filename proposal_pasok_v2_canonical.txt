Pasok: Agentic AI for FMCG Distributors
Agentic AI Hackathon 2026 · Track: Intelligence Supply Chain
Team: Vicentius Elang Krisnanto, Joshua Sahusilawane, Bintang Neera
Sistem Informasi, Universitas Katolik Atma Jaya
1. Problem statement
Demand in Indonesian general trade signals its moves early, but in a form most tools cannot read. Consider one mid-sized distributor of fast-moving consumer goods (FMCG) in Greater Jakarta: thousands of active SKUs, a sales team on daily routes to hundreds of warung, and a purchasing manager deciding what to reorder from principal brands with a spreadsheet. Traditional trade still moves roughly 69% of Indonesia's FMCG volume (Statista, 2025), nearly all through warehouses like this one. The tool is a fixed reorder point per SKU and a safety stock number copied from last year.
That tool fails in every direction: fast movers run dry before the next delivery and the sale goes elsewhere, slow movers pile up as cartons of borrowed capital, and lead times are assumed, not measured.
Above all of it moves the calendar: Ramadan and Lebaran, Imlek, THR weeks, payday cycles, principal promotions. Each wave announces itself weeks in advance as a quiet stretch of small orders, and each drifts across the Gregorian year, so "March is our best month" stops being true within a few seasons. Every SKU also carries a sellable window, months rather than years, that caps how early pre-event stock can be built; when it closes, the excess becomes expiry waste. A flat reorder point cannot see any of it.
The data to do better already sits inside the business. Sales invoices per outlet and SKU live in the order capture system, receiving records hold every principal's actual lead times, and the peaks are visible in those records. What the business lacks is a layer that reads those early signals, plans for the peak, sizes every order against shelf life, and executes. The distributor's screens stop at reports; a person still places orders weekly at best, and the wave does not wait.
2. Proposed agentic AI solution
Pasok is a four-agent system on Amazon Bedrock AgentCore that runs the distributor's replenishment loop under human supervision, with orders and stock movements flowing into the distributor's SAP business system. Each agent owns one narrow job, the way a real planning team divides the work.
1. Demand Agent. Pulls sales history per outlet and per SKU, splits the catalog into groups by sales volume and demand volatility, and produces a weekly forecast per SKU with a confidence interval.
* A calendar it looks up, and a ramp it learns per SKU. The Bedrock Knowledge Base owns an event registry whose dates drift yearly: Imlek, Lebaran, Nyepi, Christmas, THR weeks, payday cycles, principal promos. Past sales are aligned to each event's actual date, and from that alignment the agent learns when each product's demand builds, peaks, and decays, planning against this year's verified date.
* It reads early demand signals the registry does not know. When a series departs from its baseline outside any registered event, the agent drafts a candidate event for a human curator to confirm or reject, and the registry grows with the business. For a SKU with thin history, it starts from the principal's demand signal, then switches to the distributor's own outlet history.
* The buffer is explicit, per supplier, not inherited. Each order covers demand over that principal's learned lead time plus a buffer sized from that principal's actual forecast errors and lead-time variability, capped by shelf life: reliable principals run lean, erratic ones fatter.
* Hard constraints: shelf life and money. Every order respects the product's sellable window: short-lived goods arrive late, small, and staggered; when demand undershoots the curve, positions flip to expiry-risk flags with transfers or markdowns proposed while value remains. Order sizes respect each principal's credit terms and minimum order size.
* Classical models carry the numbers. Baselines come from classical statistical models, still the most reliable tool for steady, high-volume SKUs, with specialized methods for the long tail; a controlled study confirms hybrid designs lose nothing in accuracy (Tan et al., NeurIPS 2024).
2. Stock Position Agent. Maintains a live view of stock: as orders post and deliveries are received, on-hand quantities, in-transit shipments, and open purchase orders merge into one true sellable-stock figure per SKU, recomputed continuously. It computes days of cover against the forecast and flags SKUs slipping under coverage floors or drifting toward expiry risk, so problems surface within hours of the transaction that caused them, not at the next review.
3. Supplier Reliability Agent. Reads each principal's real lead time and fill rate from receiving records, notices drift, and updates the lead time the Decision Agent plans against.
4. Decision Agent. The orchestrator. For every exception SKU it weighs holding cost, service level, supplier risk, credit headroom, shelf life, and the event ramp, then picks an action: auto-issue the order, hold one cycle, redirect excess to a branch depot, or escalate. Every decision carries a plain-language rationale.
The agentic loop (autonomous multi-step execution)
A planning run is a closed loop with five steps: perceive, forecast, decide, issue, learn. The Demand, Stock Position, and Supplier Reliability agents read the catalog's current state. The Decision Agent reasons over each exception SKU, drafts the purchase order in the distributor's ordering system, and notifies the principal; when the delivery lands, forecast is compared against actuals and the next cycle starts smarter.
The agents coordinate through AgentCore's multi-agent collaboration; Memory keeps a per-SKU decision history so the system can check whether its last call was right; Gateway secures every tool call at platform level. A full pass takes minutes, on schedule.
One pass, concretely: the loop finds a biscuit SKU whose days of cover run out before the next delivery, eleven days before Lebaran. It weighs the learned event ramp, this principal's learned lead time, the credit left this month, and the SKU's remaining shelf life; it sizes the order from the ramp curve, issues the purchase order, and records its rationale.
Order stability guardrails
An autonomous reorder agent has one well-documented failure mode: the bullwhip effect (Lee et al., 1997), named the *agent bullwhip effect* in recent MIT Beer Game work (Long et al., 2026). We treat that finding as a design constraint, answered by three fixed decision rules that run while the agent works. Forecasts are smoothed before they drive sizing, quantities are dampened against the previous order, and each order is capped so variance leaving the warehouse stays near the variance coming in. Event uplifts sit outside the damping, a pre-event order is large by design, but the shelf-life cap bounds them.
Figure 1 shows the guardrails working. In a 250-day simulation built on the official Bank Indonesia retail sales index for food, beverages and tobacco, the same forecasting agent places orders two ways. Unguarded, it reacts to every daily fluctuation and its order stream swings 2.68 times wider than demand: the bullwhip in action. Guarded, orders swing only 0.24 times the demand variance, fill rate within half a point, because the rules smooth decisions without hoarding stock.

Supervised autonomy
Routine reorders inside policy limits execute automatically, learned event ramps included: ahead of Lebaran, the agent sizes the pre-event order from the curve it learned and issues it itself. The Decision Agent escalates only when stakes are high: an order above a rupiah threshold, a supplier switch, a candidate event, a demand shock nothing explains. The manager gets a short queue of exceptions with rationales instead of a wall of spreadsheet rows: supervision, not approval gating, no human in the path for most SKUs.
3. Expected impact
We measure this against one pilot: the distributor above, full catalog, over the Road to Glory window, scored against last year's quarter.
For the distributor
* Fewer stockouts on A-class SKUs, without expiry waste on the other side. Daily per-SKU review catches the buildup weeks before the peak; retail research reports similar reductions (Syed et al., 2026).
* Lower carrying cost, less waste. Volatility-matched safety stock releases capital from slow movers, and shelf-life-bounded ordering means excess that used to expire never gets bought.
* More service on the same capital. Fill rate climbs without raising inventory value, the cost-versus-service trade-off now explicit per SKU.
For the purchasing manager: a small daily exception queue instead of thousands of spreadsheet rows, hours back for principal negotiations and registry curation, and a written rationale on every auto-issued order.
